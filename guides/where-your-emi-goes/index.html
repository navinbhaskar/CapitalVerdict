Where your EMI goes

In the early years of a home loan most of each EMI is interest. Here is the arithmetic, and what a yearly prepayment does to it.

20 October 2026, 2 min read

In short

  • On a ₹40 lakh loan at 9% over 20 years, the EMI is ₹35,989, and ₹30,000 of the first one is interest.
  • Over 20 years you pay about ₹46.37 lakh of interest: more than you borrowed.
  • An extra ₹1 lakh a year clears the loan in year 13 and cuts the interest to about ₹27.97 lakh.

An EMI (equated monthly instalment) is the same amount every month, but what it pays for changes. Early on, most of it is interest. Late in the loan, most of it repays what you borrowed.

The formula

Banks quote a yearly rate and charge a twelfth of it each month. If r is that monthly rate and n the number of months:

EMI = loan × r × (1 + r)ⁿ ÷ ((1 + r)ⁿ − 1)

For ₹40 lakh at 9% over 20 years, r = 0.75% and n = 240, which gives an EMI of ₹35,989.

Month one

Interest is charged on what you owe: ₹40,00,000 × 0.75% = ₹30,000. Only the remaining ₹5,989 of the first EMI repays the loan. That is why the amount you owe falls so slowly at first.

Over the whole loan

  • 240 EMIs of ₹35,989 add up to about ₹86.37 lakh.
  • So the interest is about ₹46.37 lakh, more than the ₹40 lakh borrowed.
  • After 15 of the 20 years you still owe about ₹17.34 lakh.

What a yearly prepayment does

Paying a little extra each year, while keeping the EMI the same, cuts the interest sharply, because every rupee prepaid stops attracting interest for the rest of the loan. In our rent vs buy tool, paying an extra ₹1 lakh at the end of each year on the loan above clears it in year 13 instead of year 20, and the interest paid falls to about ₹27.97 lakh.

That extra money has to come from somewhere, of course. The tool counts it as part of what the owner pays, so the comparison with renting stays fair: the renter gets to invest the same amount.
