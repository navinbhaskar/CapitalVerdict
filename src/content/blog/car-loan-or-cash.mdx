If you have the money for a car, should you pay cash or take a loan and keep your savings invested? The answer comes down to one comparison: **does your money earn more, after tax, than the loan really costs?**

## The loan's true cost

The quoted rate is not the whole cost. A processing fee means you receive less than you borrow, but still repay the full amount. Take an ₹8 lakh loan at 9% over 5 years with a ₹5,000 fee:

- EMI **₹16,607**, and **₹1.96 lakh** of interest over the 5 years.
- Counting the fee, the loan really costs **9.67%** a year. That is the return your savings need to beat.

## Cash or loan, worked through

A ₹10 lakh car. One buyer pays cash. The other pays 20% down and the fee, takes the ₹8 lakh loan above, and keeps the rest invested. To keep it fair, the cash buyer invests every month the EMI the other buyer pays. Savings earn an illustrative 7% a year after tax.

After 5 years, both own the same car and owe nothing. **Paying cash comes out ₹67,298 ahead.** If savings earned 10% a year after tax instead, the loan would come out ₹8,927 ahead. The switch happens at 9.67%.

## Watch for flat rates

Some car loans are quoted at a **flat rate**, which is worked out on the full amount for the whole term, even though you repay it month by month. It sounds much cheaper than it is.

A "flat 5%" on ₹8 lakh over 5 years means ₹2 lakh of interest (5% × ₹8 lakh × 5 years) and an EMI of **₹16,667**. That is about the same as a **9.15%** loan on the usual reducing balance. So always ask for the reducing-balance rate, and compare that.

## Beyond the numbers

- **Keep an emergency fund.** Paying cash is not worth it if it empties your safety net.
- **Will you really keep the savings invested?** The loan only wins if the money stays invested for the whole loan.
- **Offers.** Dealers sometimes give discounts only on loans, or only on cash; count them in.
- **The car is pledged to the lender** until the loan is repaid.
