<InShort>

- On a ₹40 lakh loan at 9% over 20 years, a 1% rise after three years adds **₹2,342** to the EMI.
- If the EMI stays the same instead, the loan runs **3 years 6 months** longer and costs **₹14.77 lakh** more in interest.
- After a 3% rise, the old EMI no longer covers the interest, and the loan would never be repaid.

</InShort>

A floating-rate home loan moves with the lender's benchmark. When the rate goes up, one of two things has to give: the EMI or the length of the loan. Which one changes makes a large difference to what you pay.

## The loan

Take the loan from our guide [Where your EMI goes](/guides/where-your-emi-goes/): ₹40 lakh at 9% over 20 years. The EMI is **₹35,989**, and over 20 years the interest comes to about **₹46.37 lakh**.

After three years of EMIs, you still owe about **₹37.54 lakh**. Now the rate rises to 10%.

## Way one: the EMI goes up

The lender works out a new EMI that clears ₹37.54 lakh at 10% in the 17 years left. It is **₹38,331**, ₹2,342 a month more. Over the whole loan, the interest rises to about **₹51.15 lakh**, ₹4.78 lakh more than with no change.

As a rough guide, on a 20-year loan each 1% on the rate adds about 7% to the EMI. Had the rate been 10% from the first month, the EMI would have been ₹38,601.

## Way two: the EMI stays, the loan gets longer

If the EMI stays at ₹35,989, less of each one repays the loan, because more goes on interest. The loan then runs **23 years 6 months** instead of 20: 3 years 6 months longer. The interest comes to about **₹61.15 lakh**, which is **₹14.77 lakh** more than with no change, about three times the ₹4.78 lakh extra in way one.

The extra years cost so much because every month of them is a month of interest on what is still owed.

## Bigger rises

A 2% rise, to 11%, raises the EMI to ₹40,740. Keep the EMI instead and the loan runs 31 years 7 months, with about ₹96.20 lakh of interest.

At a 3% rise, to 12%, the interest on ₹37.54 lakh is ₹37,535 a month. That is more than the ₹35,989 EMI, so with the EMI unchanged, what you owe would grow every month. The EMI has to rise, to ₹43,211, for the loan to end at all.

## Floating or fixed?

A fixed rate removes this risk, at a price if it starts higher than the floating rate. You can work out how far the floating rate would have to rise for the fixed one to come out cheaper.

On the same loan, a fixed **10%** for all 20 years costs as much interest as a floating 9% that rises to **10.31%** after three years and stays there. A fixed 9.5% matches a floating rate that rises to 9.65%. If you expect rates to rise by more than that, and soon, the fixed rate wins; if they stay put or fall, floating does.

Rates rarely move once and stay put, so treat these as a yardstick, not a forecast.

## Before you choose

- Ask your lender what happens when the rate changes: does the EMI rise, or the loan get longer, and can you choose?
- If you are offered a fixed rate, check in the loan agreement how long it stays fixed, and what it changes to after that.
- Check the charge, if any, for switching from one to the other later.
- If the loan gets longer after a rise, a prepayment or a higher EMI can pull the end date back. The [EMI calculator](/tools/emi-calculator/) shows the effect of a yearly prepayment.
